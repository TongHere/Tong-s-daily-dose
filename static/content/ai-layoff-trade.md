# AI Layoff Trade

**Date:** May 10, 2026

**Authors:**
- Brett Hemenway Falk — Computer and Information Science, University of Pennsylvania
- Gerry Tsoukalas — Boston University Questrom

**Source:** [The AI Layoff Trap — arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20617)

---

![AI Layoff Trade Diagram](images/ai-layoff-trade-diagram.png)

Recent trends show AI automation driving massive workforce reductions, such as Block cutting nearly half of its 10,000-person staff and Salesforce laying off 4,000 workers to transition toward agentic AI. 

Currently, AI often serves as a substitute for labor rather than a reinstating complement. For an individual firm, this automation is highly attractive because it drastically reduces production costs and allows the firm to capture a larger share of expenditure, potentially increasing revenue. 
However, firms are trapped in a competitive "arms race" where no one will stop; even with perfect foresight of the coming "demand cliff," rational firms find that automating is a strictly dominant strategy.  

While the negative influence of a single firm's layoffs on aggregate demand is too small for that firm to feel individually, the collective impact is devastating. When all companies automate simultaneously, the workers—who are also the customers—lose their purchasing power and can no longer spend money in the economy. This leads to a self-destructive cycle where both company revenues and people's incomes collapse, resulting in a deadweight loss that harms both owners and workers.  

The effective solution could be a Pigouvian automation tax, which targets the root of the problem by taxing every instance of automation. By doing so, the policy forces companies to internalize the external costs of demand destruction, aligning private incentives with the collective survival of the market.
